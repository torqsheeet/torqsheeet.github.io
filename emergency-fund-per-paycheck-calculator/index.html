Emergency fund calculator: what to save from each paycheck

An emergency fund is cash set aside for the month that goes wrong: a lost job, a car that will not start, a boiler, a vet bill. A common rule of thumb is three to six months of essential costs (rent or mortgage, utilities, food, insurance, minimum debt payments, transport), not of everything you spend. Big round targets are hard to start on, so this calculator breaks yours into paydays: what to move on each one to reach the target by a date, or when a fixed amount per paycheck gets you there.


Runs in your browser; nothing is sent anywhere. A payday on the target date counts. Weekend and holiday shifts are not applied. Interest on the savings is ignored, so a savings account that pays interest gets you there a little sooner.

How the numbers work

Target = essential monthly costs × months covered. Still to save = target minus what you already have. Per paycheck = still to save divided by the paydays from your next one up to the target date. Finish date = the payday on which a fixed amount per paycheck first covers what is left (still to save divided by the amount, rounded up, counted in paydays).

Example with the starting figures: 2,500 of essential costs a month, three months covered, 500 already saved. The target is 7,500, so 7,000 is still to save. Paid every two weeks, a year holds 26 paydays, so it is about 269 a paycheck to get there in twelve months; at 150 a paycheck it takes 47 paydays, about 21 months.

Making the number smaller

  • Count essential costs only. A fund that covers rent, food and bills is enough to keep an emergency off a credit card; it does not have to cover the gym.
  • Start with one month. A starter fund handles most single surprises (a repair, a deposit); build from there.
  • Put windfalls in: a tax refund, a bonus, or the extra paycheck in a three-paycheck month (and some people get 27 paychecks in 2027).
  • Keep known yearly bills out of it. Insurance renewals and Christmas are not emergencies; give them their own sinking funds so the emergency fund is not spent on them.
  • If you also carry high-interest debt, many people build a starter fund first and then split extra money between the fund and the debt; see the debt snowball vs avalanche calculator.

This is general budgeting information, not financial advice. How big a fund you need depends on your job, household and other savings.
